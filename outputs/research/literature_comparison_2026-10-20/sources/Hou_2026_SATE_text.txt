Developmental language disorder affects approximately 7-10% of children in the United States and is associated with long-
term difficulties in literacy, academic achievement, and social participation. Language sample analysis (LSA) is widely re-
garded as a gold-standard approach for evaluating children’s language development; yet, it remains underused in clinical and
research practice because it depends on labor-intensive and time-consuming manual transcription and coding. Therefore, we
propose SATE, a scalable and automated system for child language assessment. It implements a modular machine learning
pipeline that reproduces the full LSA workflow, including verbatim transcription, utterance segmentation, speaker identifica-
tion, maze annotation, lexical and grammatical analyses, and language metrics computation. The pipeline is complemented
by a user interface that supports efficient review and correction of model output, allowing researchers and clinicians to val-
idate and refine automated results. Experimental evaluation on child speech corpora demonstrates that SATE substantially
outperforms existing baselines, achieving 11.81% word error rate and strong correlations with authenticated results of lan-
guage metrics. A counterbalanced human-in-the-loop study showed that SATE-assisted LSA reduced task completion time
by 55.3% while maintaining comparable accuracy to manual LSA, and usability questionnaires yielded a mean SUS score of
81.25. These results provide evidence that SATE is a practical tool for child language sample analysis.

1 INTRODUCTION
Developmental language disorder (DLD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent diffi-
culties in the acquisition and use of language that cannot be explained by hearing loss, intellectual disability,
or other biomedical conditions. Large epidemiological studies have estimated prevalence around 7% to 10% of
school-age children, underscoring its public health relevance [4, 5, 31, 42]. Children with DLD are at increased
risk for reading difficulties, reduced educational attainment, and long-term impacts on employment and mental
health [29, 46]. However, only about 30% of children with DLD have been identified [29]. Thus, there is a sus-
tained need for methods that can reliably evaluate children’s language development and support both clinical
decision making and research on mechanisms and interventions.
Language sample analysis (LSA) occupies a central position in this landscape. Unlike standardized language
tests, which typically elicit constrained responses, LSA examines children’s spontaneous language use in real-
life situations, such as conversation or story telling. This approach enables detailed profiling of children’s lexical,
morphological, syntactic, and discourse skills, as well as the disfluencies they produce. Research has consistently
shown that LSA measures can help clinicians diagnose children with DLD and identify intervention goals [ 11,
32, 44]. As a result, LSA is widely regarded as a gold-standard method for child language assessment.
Despite the usefulness of LSA, it is still not conducted regularly in clinical settings partly due to the time
constraint. For example, a conversational language sample of 15 to 20 minutes is typically needed for a preschool
child to obtain the recommended sample length, such as 50 to 100 utterances. Typically, a one-minute language
sample takes an experienced SLP 5 to 8 minutes to process. Thus, it may take approximately 1 to 3 hours to process
the language sample for a preschooler. Surveys of practicing SLPs report substantial barriers to the routine use
of LSA, including the extensive time required for transcription and coding, the need for specialized training, and
concerns about scoring consistency [19, 45]. These challenges are further compounded by a persistent shortage
of SLPs and their increasing workloads, which limit the feasibility of applying LSA in clinical practice.
A central promise of ubiquitous computing in education is support that reaches learners wherever learning
happens, in classrooms, therapy rooms, and homes. For learners facing language challenges, this support remains
largely out of reach: most children with DLD are never identified, and for those who are, progress monitoring
depends on hours of specialist coding per sample. Diagnosis therefore comes late, and improvement is rarely
measured. Removing this analysis bottleneck is a prerequisite for educational environments that can detect,
diagnose, and track language difficulties as part of everyday practice.
Existing computational approaches only partially address these limitations. Existing LSA tools such as CLAN
[26] and SALT [16] support standardized coding conventions and metric computation, but they generally assume
that transcripts and annotations have already been produced by trained human coders. General-purpose and
child-adapted ASR systems can reduce part of the transcription burden, but they are not designed to preserve
all LSA-relevant phenomena or to produce editable intermediate annotations such as C-unit boundaries, maze
labels, speaker labels, and morpheme codes. Existing automated LSA systems also provide limited support for
expert verification and correction. Collectively, these gaps highlight the need for a comprehensive, efficient, and
verifiable approach to automating Language Sample Analysis while preserving efficient expert oversight.
Therefore, we propose SATE (Speech Annotation and Transcription Enhancer), a human-in-the-loop system
for automated child LSA. SATE combines three design choices. First, it implements a domain-adapted pipeline

• A verifiable LSA automation system that directly addresses a long-standing barrier in speech pathol-

representations, the system enables reliable LSA at scale.

performance comparable under ASR discrepancies, demonstrating joint modeling improved robustness to
upstream transcription errors.
• A human-in-the-loop interaction paradigm that improves the usability of automated LSA, integrating
audio-synchronized review with multi-layer visualization and efficient editing of transcripts and linguistic
annotations. This is the first system that couples an automated LSA pipeline with an expert-facing correc-
tion interface, and we provide preliminary human-in-the-loop evidence of its practical utility through a
pilot user study.
2 BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
In this section, Section 2.1 first introduces the LSA workflow and the task requirements that motivate the system
design. Sections 2.2–2.3 then review related work on children’s ASR and component-level automation. Finally,
Section 2.4 discusses existing automated LSA systems and the remaining gap for verifiable human-in-the-loop
analysis.
2.1 Language Sample Analysis Workflow and Automation Requirements
A typical manual LSA workflow is illustrated in Figure 1. A trained SLP first listens to a child language sample
and produces a verbatim orthographic transcript. The transcript is then segmented into communication units
(C-units), speaker turns are identified, and disfluencies are annotated as mazes. Inflectional morphemes are
subsequently coded at the word level, and summary language metrics are computed. These metrics are then
interpreted relative to age expectations, diagnostic needs, and intervention goals.
Several LSA conventions are especially important for automation. A C-unit is commonly defined as an in-
dependent clause together with any dependent clauses that belong to it [ 24]. This definition differs from ordi-
nary sentence segmentation because children’s spontaneous speech often contains incomplete clauses, coordina-
tion, noncanonical grammar, and disfluencies. Maze annotation identifies repetitions, revisions, and filler words.
These segments are clinically meaningful because they may reflect language planning and repair processes, but
they are also excluded from some downstream linguistic metrics so that repetitions or abandoned forms do not

This workflow creates two requirements that are not addressed by ordinary transcription systems. First, the

rather than normalizing them into fluent adult-like sentences. Second, the system must expose intermediate
representations for review, because errors in transcription, segmentation, maze annotation, or morpheme coding
can change final LSA metrics. These requirements motivate the design of SATE as an automated but verifiable
workflow rather than a fully automatic black-box predictor.
2.2 Children’s Automatic Speech Recognition and Speech Assessment
Verbatim transcription of child speech is the foundation of automated LSA, yet children’s ASR remains a difficult
problem. Compared with adult speech, children’s speech contains greater acoustic variability, higher pitch, less
stable articulation, developing phonology, invented words, frequent disfluencies, and noncanonical grammatical
constructions [14]. These properties produce a persistent domain mismatch for ASR models trained primarily on
adult fluent speech. Language model priors can further normalize or suppress precisely the atypical productions
that LSA needs to preserve, including repetitions, revisions, omissions, and morphological errors [41].
Recent work has therefore explored child-specific adaptation of modern ASR systems. Rolland and Abad stud-
ied transfer-based strategies for improving children’s ASR, including adaptation methods designed to address
the acoustic variability and data scarcity of child speech [36]. Jain et al. examined native and non-native English
child speech recognition with Whisper and showed that fine-tuning large pretrained models on child speech
can substantially improve performance on child ASR benchmarks [18]. Attia et al. similarly demonstrated that
child-oriented ASR adaptation can reduce recognition errors in children’s speech [ 2]. In adjacent educational
assessment work, Wang et al. proposed a unified model for oral reading fluency and student prosody, illustrating
the broader movement toward task-specific child speech technologies for educational and language assessment
contexts [43].

2.3 Component-Level Automation for LSA Annotations
Speech and language processing research has developed models for transcription, disfluency detection, segmen-
tation, and linguistic analysis. However, these components remain poorly suited for child LSA due to domain
mismatch and error propagation.
Disfluency and Maze Detection. Maze annotation is related to disfluency detection in NLP and speech process-
ing. Early text-based methods relied on surface cues such as repeated words [ 23], while more recent approaches
use contextual representations from bidirectional LSTM and Transformer architectures [25, 48]. Audio-based
and audio-text methods use prosodic cues and acoustic-textual fusion to improve detection [ 12, 37, 39]. Recent
work has also explored generating disfluency tokens during speech transcription [49]. However, most disfluency
systems are designed for adult conversational speech or generic disfluency labels, not for child LSA conventions.
In child LSA, maze annotations must be compatible with SALT/CHAT-style coding and must operate reliably on
spontaneous language from children with and without DLD.
Applying existing disfluency methods directly to LSA therefore presents three challenges. First, text-only
models inherit upstream ASR errors. Second, audio-only models require alignment back to transcript tokens
before LSA metrics can be computed. Third, child speech and DLD speech contain disfluency, grammar, and
phonological patterns that differ from adult corpora [5]. These challenges motivate a domain-adapted maze
classifier and the end-to-end transcription-and-maze models evaluated in this paper.
Utterance and C-unit Segmentation. Sentence segmentation models divide text into sentence-like units for
downstream NLP tasks [13, 30]. C-unit segmentation differs from ordinary sentence boundary detection be-
cause it follows clinical LSA conventions rather than written punctuation or adult sentence structure. Batchalign
includes C-unit-oriented processing for CHAT transcripts [22], but few segmentation systems are trained specif-
ically for children’s spontaneous language. Disfluencies, incomplete utterances, and noncanonical grammar are
especially common in child speech and can degrade generic segmentation models.
Morphological Analysis. Morphological analysis has long been supported by tools such as CLAN and its MOR
program [26]. Neural NLP toolkits such as Stanza provide tokenization, lemmatization, POS tagging, dependency
parsing, and Universal Dependencies morphological features [9, 33]. However, LSA morpheme coding differs
from generic morphological tagging because it must map linguistic features to clinically meaningful inflectional
morpheme categories. In addition, mazes can disrupt the local syntactic context used by morphological analyzers.
For this reason, SATE performs morpheme annotation after maze detection and exclusion.
2.4 Automated Language Sample Analysis Systems
Batchalign [22] is a major effort toward automated LSA. It can generate CHAT-format [26] transcripts from
speech and connects ASR with downstream transcript processing. For the clinical and research use case targeted
by SATE, however, two limitations remain important.
Limitation 1: Sequential pipeline without mitigation of error accumulation. Batchalign integrates existing mod-
els into a sequential pipeline where each stage operates independently. Although the authors note that low ASR

In summary, prior work establishes the importance of child-specific ASR adaptation and provides component
technologies for disfluency detection, segmentation, and morphological analysis. However, existing approaches
do not fully address the combined requirements of child LSA: preserving clinically meaningful verbatim pro-
ductions, producing structured LSA annotations, reducing error propagation, and supporting expert verification.
SATE addresses this gap through domain-adapted models, end-to-end transcription-and-maze architectures, and
an interactive interface for efficient human oversight.
3 DATASET AND PREPROCESSING
All training and evaluation data used in this study is from the Edmonton Narrative Norms Instrument (ENNI)
dataset [38]. The ENNI corpus was collected to support the assessment of children’s narrative language abilities
and contains spontaneous speech samples from 361 children aged 4 to 9 years, including both typically devel-
oping (TD) children and children diagnosed with DLD. In the dataset, children were prompted to describe short
stories composed of 3 sequential pictures from 2 narrative types that introduce linguistic variety.
The ENNI dataset was transcribed in 2 formats. One widely used format is the Codes for the Human Analysis
of Transcripts (CHAT), which is publicly available through the TalkBank [26] CHILDES [27] database. CHAT
transcriptions encode maze using symbolic annotations (e.g., <, >, [/], or [//]), and each transcription line cor-
responds to a single C-unit with associated speaker labels and time stamps. For example, a CHAT line such as
*CHI: <he was> [/] he was running. indicates that the utterance was produced by the child speaker, that
the entire line is one C-unit, and that <he was> [/] is a repetition maze. Similarly, *CHI: <he is> [//] he
was happy. represents a revision maze, while speaker prefixes such as *CHI: and *INV: distinguish child and
examiner utterances. As a result, the CHAT format offers reliable reference annotations for evaluating verbatim
transcription, maze detection, speaker identification, and C-unit segmentation.
However, the inflectional morpheme annotations are generated automatically by the Computerized Language
Analysis (CLAN) software as dependent tiers rather than being manually annotated and verified by experts.
Therefore, we did not use the CHAT-format transcripts for evaluating morpheme annotation performance. We
incorporated a second version of the ENNI transcripts in Systematic Analysis of Language Transcripts (SALT)
format, obtained from prior work [ 15]. In this dataset, all inflectional morphemes were manually annotated by
licensed SLPs, and reliable LSA metrics were computed based on these expert-coded transcripts. The SALT ver-
sion contains the same language samples as the CHAT version, and its orthographic transcriptions and C-unit
boundaries are consistent with the CHAT transcripts. The main difference is that the SALT version includes
expert-verified inflectional morpheme coding. For example, SALT marks bound morphemes using suffix anno-
tations appended to word roots, such as dog/s for plural -s, walk/ed for past tense -ed, walk/ing for present
participle -ing, and walk/3s for third-person singular -s. This SALT-format dataset was therefore used for eval-
uating morpheme annotation and for assessing the overall system performance of SATE.

4 SATE: SPEECH ANNOTATION AND TRANSCRIPTION ENHANCER
Our SATE system consists of two mutually reinforcing components as shown in Figure 2. The first component
is an automated machine learning pipeline that reproduces the Language Sample Analysis (LSA) workflow, in-
cluding verbatim transcription, utterance segmentation into communication units, speaker identification, maze
annotation, morphological analysis, and computation of language metrics. The second component is an interac-
tive web-based environment that presents model outputs in an interpretable form and enables efficient review,
audio-synchronized verification, and correction of model predictions. Together, these components form an inte-
grated system that balances automation with human oversight, ensuring both efficiency and analytic reliability.
This section is organized as follows. Section 4.1 describes our pipeline solution, in which each analytic task is
addressed by a individual machine learning model operating in sequence. Section 4.2 presents two end-to-end
modeling approaches that unify transcription and annotation within a single model, thereby mitigating the error
propagation in sequential pipelines. Finally, Section 4.4 introduces the design of the user interface, including
the review panel layout, annotation display, and editing operations that support efficient human-in-the-loop
verification and correction.

4.1 Pipeline Solution of Automatic Language Sample Analysis
In the pipeline solution, SATE implements a sequence of independent models that mirror each step of the LSA
workflow. Each component addresses a well-defined subtask and produces structured outputs that serve as in-
puts to subsequent stages. The upper half of Figure 3 illustrates how raw audio is progressively transformed
into LSA metrics through successive model inference steps. This pipeline formulation emphasizes transparency,
modularity, and interpretability, and each stage can be independently developed, evaluated and updated.
4.1.1 Verbatim Transcription Model. To develop an ASR system that is accurate for children’s speech while
preserving everything that is actually spoken in verbatim transcription, SATE adopts a supervised full-parameter
fine-tuning strategy using spontaneous child speech dataset that corresponding transcripts preserve speech
maze and linguistically relevant deviations, such as morphological errors. This fine-tuning strategy ensures that
the ASR model is not only acoustically adapted to children’s speech, but also linguistically aligned with the
requirements of downstream LSA tasks.
The ASR component is initialized from the publicly released openai/whisper-small checkpoint [ 34]. All
encoder and decoder parameters are updated during fine-tuning, with no layers frozen. Audio is resampled to
16 kHz and converted to log-mel features using the backbone’s standard preprocessing. Training hyperparame-
ters and decoding settings are listed in Section 4.3.
At inference time, this module takes a complete speech sample audio file as input and produces a continuous
verbatim transcript of the entire recording. The output is not segmented at this stage, and maze structures within
the transcript are not identified. These analyses are performed by downstream models in the SATE pipeline.

operates directly on the textual output and is formulated as a token-level binary classification task, in which

The segmentation model is initialized from bert-base-uncased [10] with a linear classification head over

boundaries in the presence of disfluent child speech. Training hyperparameters are listed in Section 4.3.
4.1.3 Speaker Identification. Language samples often involve interactions between children and adults (e.g., par-
ent, SLP). Thus, distinguishing between speakers is essential for computing metrics for the child specifically, as
well as for identifying interaction dynamics. SATE performs speaker identification by combining time alignment
with acoustic classification models. Forced alignment is first performed using a wav2vec2-CTC based aligner,
which provides temporal localization of each word and yields the start and end time boundaries of each C-unit.
These boundaries are then used to segment the full recording into corresponding C-unit audio segments. Each
audio segment is passed to the bookbot/wav2vec2-adult-child-cls model, an off-the-shelf wav2vec 2.0 audio
classification model designed for adult and child speech classification. This two-stage approach enables SATE
to classify speakers at the span of each C-unit using acoustic features, improving accuracy while maintaining
alignment between utterance-level and speaker-level segmentation.
We do not introduce a novel speaker identification model in this work. Our contribution in this component
lies not in model innovation, but in the systematic integration of speaker identification into the automated LSA
workflow, ensuring reliable alignment with transcription, segmentation, and subsequent linguistic analyses.

4.1.5 Morphological Analysis. Rather than developing a new morphological analysis model, our goal is to re-
liably integrate established linguistic analysis tools into an automated LSA workflow. Accordingly, SATE uses
the Stanza natural language processing toolkit [33], which provides universal part-of-speech tags, language-
specific POS tags, and rich morphological feature annotations based on the Universal Dependencies framework
[9]. Stanza has been validated across a wide range of corpora and languages, making it a stable foundation for
morphological analysis.
To align Stanza outputs with the inflectional morpheme categories defined in LSA, we implement a conver-
sion pipeline that maps Universal Dependencies features such as Number, Tense, and Person to the six target
morpheme types. This conversion process reproduces the morpheme coding decisions made in manual LSA
and yields word-level morpheme labels that can be directly used for metric computation. Importantly, morpho-
logical analysis in SATE is performed only after maze has been detected and ignored from the transcriptions.
Mazes such as repetitions and revisions often disrupt local syntactic context and can introduce spurious cues for
morphological analyzers. By excluding mazes prior to morphological processing, SATE ensures that inflectional
features are derived from linguistically well-formed utterances, resulting in more stable and reliable morpheme
annotation. Experimental results demonstrate that this design substantially improves the accuracy as shown in
Table 6.
4.1.6 Language Sample Analysis Metrics. Once transcription and all processing are complete, SATE computes
the LSA metrics widely used in both clinical and research practice. Core measures include the number of total
utterances (NTU), number of total words (NTW), number of different words (NDW), and mean length of utter-
ance in morphemes (MLUm). All metric definitions and calculation procedures strictly follow SALT conventions
[16], which is the most widely adopted standard among SLPs [45]. In addition to these core measures, SATE also
computes lexical diversity metrics, including Type–Token Ratio (TTR) [ 35] and Vocabulary Diversity (VOC-D)
[28], both of which have been extensively validated in LSA research.
The computation of these metrics marks the completion of the LSA workflow. In clinical practice, SLPs typi-
cally compare a child’s metrics with normative values from age-matched peers to support diagnostic decisions, or
track changes in these metrics over time to evaluate the progress of intervention. For researchers, these metrics
serve as the foundational starting point for quantitative analyses of children’s language development.

performance as transcription errors increase, illustrating the compounding effect of upstream inaccuracies on
downstream tasks. To address this limitation, we adopt an end-to-end modeling strategy that jointly performs

4.2.1 End-to-end Solution 1: Inline Label Generation via Special Tokens. Special tokens have been widely used
in transformer-based text modeling to encode structural and control information, such as sentence boundary
markers, padding tokens, task identifiers, and prompt-conditioning tokens. These tokens allow models to incor-
porate auxiliary signals into the generation process without altering the core architecture, effectively guiding
decoding behavior through learned symbolic representations.
In speech modeling, especially in automatic speech recognition systems, special tokens are adopted to control
transcription behavior. For example, Whisper [34] introduces language tokens to specify the target transcription
language, as well as voice activity tokens to suppress transcription in non-speech segments. Beyond these uses,
special tokens have also been explored for timestamp prediction [17], speaker diarization [20], and sound event
detection [47]. Although special-token approaches have been studied in speech and text modeling, their applica-
tion to disfluency and maze annotation in LSA remains limited. One of the few existing works [ 49] benchmarks
speech disfluency detection using token prediction during transcription. However, that work relies on synthetic
training data and does not preserve LSA-compatible lexical transcripts, limiting its applicability for real-world
language analysis.

and accuracy of maze detection.

with models trained to predict text tokens alone, as shown in Table 4. To address this limitation, we propose a

4.3 Implementation Details
To support reproducibility, we summarize the main training settings for each learnable component in Table 3.
All models were trained on the ENNI training partition described in Section 3. The training partition was further
split into 90% training and 10% validation data for checkpoint selection. Unless otherwise stated, models were
optimized with AdamW, linear warmup followed by linear decay, gradient clipping with maximum norm 1.0,
and mixed-precision training when supported. A fixed random seed of 42 was used for ASR and end-to-end
model training. No layers were frozen for the fine-tuned ASR, BERT-based, or end-to-end models. The speaker
identification model, morpheme annotation were used off the shelf and was not fine-tuned.
For the pipeline maze classifier, binary cross-entropy was applied independently to repetition, revision, and
filled-pause labels, with a threshold of 0.5 at inference. For E2E Solution 2, the multi-task objective was ℒ =
0.5 ℒASR + 0.5ℒmaze, where ℒmaze used focal loss with 𝛾 = 2.0 and uniform 𝛼 = 1.0 . During inference, Whisper-
based models used greedy decoding with beam size 1, temperature 0.0, length penalty 1.0, and timestamp pre-
diction disabled. E2E Solution 1 additionally disabled forced decoder prompts and token suppression to allow
generation of the added maze tokens.

that the final results are trustworthy. In the SATE workflow, users begin by uploading audio recordings to the
server, where a deployed machine learning pipeline automatically processes it and returns structured results to

Prior work shows that usability, cognitive load, and interface transparency strongly affect experts’ ability to
efficiently and reliably validate system outputs, particularly in multimodal transcription and annotation tasks
involving both audio and text [8, 40]. Guided by these principles, SATE incorporates an audio-synchronized and
annotation-aware interface. The design focuses on two objectives: presenting transcriptions and maze annota-
tions in a manner that is transparent, to support rapid inspection and comprehension, and to enable efficient
editing operations that allow users to correct errors with minimal cognitive load. These two design emphases
are addressed in the following sections.
4.4.1 Review Panel Design. The review panel is organized into three coordinated regions: a navigation panel on
the left, a transcription panel in the center and a language analysis panel on the right, as illustrated in Figure
5. The central transcription panel consists of vertically stacked units corresponding to individual C-units. Each
unit displays the speaker label, the orthographic transcript and any annotations such as maze or inflectional mor-
pheme. During audio playback, the active C-unit and word are highlighted in synchronize, which significantly
reduces search time and facilitates the human detection of transcription or annotation errors.
Within each C-unit, maze and morpheme annotations are visually distinguished using different highlight
styles, enabling users to recognize annotation types at a glance while preserving readability of the transcript.
Additional details remain accessible: when a user selects an annotated token, the corresponding metadata, such
as maze category or morpheme suffix, is popped up on demand, supporting closer inspection without cluttering
the primary view. The right side analysis panel include LSA metrics computed from the current transcript and
selected speaker, updating dynamically in response to user edits.
4.4.2 Editing Operations. To support efficient and reliable human-in-the-loop correction, SATE provides two
types of editing operations that align with the hierarchical structure of LSA transcription: transcription and
annotation editing and unit editing, as illustrated in Figure 5.
Transcription and annotation editing enables revision of lexical content and maze, morpheme labels. When
a user double-clicks a C-unit, the interface transitions into an editable text in SALT annotation format, which
uses symbolic markers to encode annotations. Users can correct transcription errors, adjust maze boundaries or
revise morphological markers. Unit editing supports modifications including splitting a C-unit, merging adjacent
units, reassigning speaker labels and adding, excluding or deleting units.
The combined design of editing functions enables users to validate and correct all intermediate output underly-
ing LSA conventions, ensuring that the final metrics results reflect expert judgment while retaining the efficiency
benefits of automation. More importantly, this design transforms SATE from an automated LSA model into a
fully usable tool for clinical and research practice.

lacks the correct label is an aligned false negative (𝐹 𝑁 aligned, a detector error), and a ground-truth morpheme
whose word was deleted by the ASR is a misaligned false negative (𝐹 𝑁 misaligned, an ASR-induced error). Sym-
metrically, remaining predictions on aligned words are aligned false positives (𝐹 𝑃 aligned), and predictions on
ASR-inserted words are misaligned false positives (𝐹 𝑃 misaligned).
Figure 13 in Appendix C visualizes this decomposition. The number of ground-truth morphemes is fixed across
ASR systems, so the larger 𝐹 𝑁misaligned segments of error-prone ASR systems directly reflect morphemes that
become unrecoverable when the corresponding words are missing from the transcript, while the prediction-side
panels separate detector over-prediction from predictions on inserted words. The decomposition thus makes
explicit how much of the downstream error is attributable to the morpheme detector versus upstream transcrip-
tion.

annotations for Ours-E2E-1 and Ours-E2E-2. This comparison tests whether excluding disfluent material im-

5.5 Usability and Human-in-the-loop Evaluation
5.5.1 Participants and Study Protocol. To evaluate SATE as a human-in-the-loop LSA system, we designed the
user study around two primary aims: (1) to evaluate whether SATE-assisted LSA reduces completion time rel-
ative to fully manual LSA, and (2) to evaluate whether SATE-assisted LSA maintains comparable accuracy af-
ter human review. The study began in February 2026. Twelve participants were recruited through a university
Speech-Language Pathology (SLP) program. Most participants were SLP graduate students, and one participant
was a licensed SLP. All participants had prior exposure to LSA conventions and SALT-based language sample
transcription or analysis.
The study used the pipeline version of SATE, which is the configuration deployed in the current interface; the
end-to-end variants were evaluated algorithmically in Section 4.2 but were not used as the source of interface
outputs here. Two short language samples from the ENNI test set were selected for the main tasks, summa-
rized in Table 11. The two samples were similar in length and linguistic complexity to support counterbalanced
comparison between the SATE-assisted and manual conditions.
Each participant completed two main tasks: one SATE-assisted LSA task and one fully manual LSA task.
In the SATE-assisted condition, participants uploaded the audio sample to SATE, reviewed the automatically
generated pipeline output, and corrected transcription, C-unit segmentation, speaker label, maze annotation, and
morpheme annotation errors when needed. In the manual condition, participants performed LSA from scratch
using SALT. Task time excluded software setup. Manual timing began when the participant started playing the
audio and entering the transcript in SALT and ended when the final transcript was saved. SATE timing began
when the participant uploaded the audio file to SATE and ended when the corrected output was saved.
We used a within-subject counterbalanced design to control for tool order and sample differences. The 12
participants were organized into three complete groups of four participants, and within each group, participants
were assigned to one of four task orders, as shown in Table 12. Thus, each group produced four SATE-assisted

Comparing SATE-reviewed with SATE-raw in Table 14 shows that human review did not uniformly improve

manual condition also shows nonzero WER against the same reference. These results suggest that the value of
human review was not to repair large systematic errors, but to verify the automated output and correct specific
remaining errors.

specific remaining errors, and on the four transcription and annotation metrics the reviewed output was as
accurate as or more accurate than fully manual LSA in this pilot study. The time savings reported above therefore
did not come at the cost of analysis accuracy on these measures.

from both the counterbalanced study and the earlier focus group, we interpret these findings as preliminary
evidence of perceived usability rather than definitive validation of clinical deployment readiness.
In addition to perceived usability, we examined the edits participants made when reviewing SATE outputs.
Edit counts were computed by comparing each participant’s final SATE-reviewed output with the corresponding
SATE-raw output, and thus reflect net changes in the reviewed transcript and annotations rather than low-level
interaction logs. We counted four types of changes: word-level transcription, C-unit segmentation, maze label,
and morpheme label edits.

outputs already provided a strong starting point for these short samples, and that human review primarily
functioned as a targeted verification and correction step rather than a full re-annotation process.

6 DISCUSSION, LIMITATION, AND FUTURE WORK
Our experimental evaluation demonstrates that SATE advances the current LSA system across multiple dimen-
sions. At the component level, the domain-adapted ASR model achieves 11.81% word error rate compared with
23.75% for Batchalign. The C-unit segmentation and maze annotation models similarly outperform baseline ap-
proaches when evaluated on children’s speech data. Beyond these objective model performance, SATE integrates
an interactive interface specifically designed for SLPs, enabling efficient review and correction of automated
outputs. The human-in-the-loop evaluation shows that the interface reduced task completion time by 55.3%,
supported targeted correction of automated outputs, and received a mean SUS score of 81.25, suggesting posi-
tive perceived usability. By combining high-performing automated models with human-in-the-loop verification
capabilities, SATE addresses the core efficiency barrier that has limited clinical adoption of LSA.
Our comparison of pipeline and end-to-end approaches reveals complementary strengths. The pipeline solu-
tion achieves superior performance on individual components with clean inputs, offering modularity and inter-
pretability. The end-to-end solutions demonstrate greater robustness to error accumulation, with substantially
smaller performance degradation as transcription errors increase. Accordingly, we recommend the pipeline ap-
proach when transcription quality is relatively high, and the end-to-end approaches for more challenging audio
conditions where higher ASR error rates are expected.
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted on the ENNI dataset, which
represents a limited sample size and a specific elicitation context. Second, agreement with SLP experts is not
uniform across LSA metrics: the count-based lexical measures NTW and NDW are relatively stable, whereas
NTU exhibited larger absolute differences from expert values, and MLUm showed the weakest agreement (𝑟 =

Clinically, the automated outputs should be treated as screening- or review-support measures rather than
replacements for expert judgment: SATE captures the overall ranking and developmental patterns of children’s
language, but residual systematic bias can distort individual-level interpretation, for example by making a child’s
language appear less syntactically mature than it is and exaggerating perceived impairment severity. SATE is
therefore best used as a human-in-the-loop system, in which automated estimates guide efficient review and
clinicians verify C-unit boundaries, maze exclusion, and morpheme labels before making diagnostic decisions.
Future work will pursue several directions. First, rigorous clinical validation studies should examine whether
SATE-assisted LSA leads to diagnostic decisions concordant with fully manual analysis while achieving signif-
icant improvements in efficiency. Second, we plan to integrate SATE with wearable recording devices to ana-
lyze children’s everyday language in the settings where it is naturally produced, shifting LSA from a one-time,
appointment-based assessment toward repeatable longitudinal measurement; in such continuous use, efficient
human-in-the-loop verification becomes an interaction design challenge as much as an accuracy one. Third,
we will extend the end-to-end architecture to jointly optimize more components such as C-unit segmentation
and morphological analysis. Finally, we aim to conduct broader system deployment and data collection efforts
to construct normative databases that can serve as standardized references for SLPs when making diagnostic
decisions.
7 CONCLUSION
This paper presented SATE (Speech Annotation and Transcription Enhancer), an automated system addressing
the efficiency barriers that have limited clinical adoption of Language Sample Analysis for child language assess-
ment. SATE implements a machine learning pipeline reproducing the complete LSA workflow and the system
is further complemented by an interactive interface supporting human-in-the-loop verification.
Our evaluation demonstrated three key findings. First, our system significantly outperforms baseline methods,
achieving a word error rate of 11.81% compared with 23.75% for existing automated LSA systems on children’s
speech. Second, end-to-end architectures for joint transcription and maze annotation reduce error accumulation,
offering improved robustness when transcription quality is degraded. Third, system-level evaluation showed
strong correlations between automated and expert-produced metrics, confirming that SATE preserves diagnos-
tically relevant patterns. A pilot human-in-the-loop evaluation provided evidence that SATE can reduce manual
workload while preserving final LSA accuracy after user review.
By transforming the labor-intensive LSA workflow into an automated pipeline with transparent, verifiable,
and editable intermediate representations, SATE makes gold-standard language assessment accessible at scale.
For the ubiquitous computing community, SATE turns children’s daily spontaneous speech into developmen-
tal measures that specialist can verify. This makes downstream applications practical: automatic analysis of
each therapy session to track a child’s progress during intervention, classroom deployment for large-scale DLD
screening, and home deployment to study how children’s language input environments shape their language
development.

A.3 SUS Scoring Procedure
SUS scores were computed following the standard scoring procedure [7]. Each of the ten SUS items was rated
on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). For positively worded items (S1, S3, S5,
S7, and S9), the item contribution was computed as the raw item score minus 1. For negatively worded items (S2,
S4, S6, S8, and S10), the item contribution was computed as 5 minus the raw item score. The ten adjusted item
contributions were then summed and multiplied by 2.5, yielding a total SUS score ranging from 0 to 100, where
higher scores indicate better perceived usability.
